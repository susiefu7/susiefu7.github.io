# The Return

The ferry came in under a low sky, and Iris stood at the rail long after the other passengers had gone below. Harrow Bay arrived the way it always had in her memory: first the lighthouse, dark and patient on its rock, then the harbour wall, then the houses climbing the hill in their faded blues and greys as if they had been set there by someone who had lost interest halfway.

She had not meant to come back in winter. She had not meant to come back at all.

On the quay a boy was coiling rope with great seriousness. He looked up at her camera bag and then at her face, and she saw him decide she was nobody in particular. It was a relief.

* * *

The key was under the third stone of the garden wall. Of course it was. Her mother had never trusted anything she could not touch, and so she had trusted stones, and kettles, and the weight of a logbook in her lap at night.

The door was unlocked anyway.

Inside, the house smelled of cold ash and lavender. Someone had been in to clear the post. Someone had left a loaf on the table, wrapped in a tea towel, and a note in a hand Iris knew before she had read a word of it.

*Welcome home. — T.*

She sat down at the table without taking off her coat.

* * *

Tomas was at the harbour office, as he had been at the harbour office for eleven years, as if the building had grown up around him. He came out before she reached the door.

"You found the bread," he said.

"I found the bread."

He smiled then, properly, the way he had when they were children and had got away with something, and she felt the eleven years lean back a little to let them both through.

"Your mother would have wanted you to have the logbooks," he said. "I kept them dry."

"Thank you," she said, and meant it, and did not yet ask why he had needed to keep them at all.
